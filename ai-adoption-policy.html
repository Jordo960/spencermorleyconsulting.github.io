A policy your council adopts.
Procedures your staff can follow.

An AI adoption policy and AI adoption procedures are two different documents doing two different jobs. The policy states the rules and who is accountable for them — it is what council or leadership formally adopts. The procedures state what people actually do: how a tool gets approved, what is logged, and what happens when something goes wrong. Spencer Morley Consulting writes both.

Where most organizations actually are

Your staff started before your policy did.

That is the ordinary case, not the negligent one. Roughly half of Canadian public servants report using AI tools at work, while only about one in five organizations has a formal AI adoption policy in place at all. The tools arrived through a browser tab rather than through procurement — nobody had to approve anything for it to start, so nobody did.

For an Alberta public body, that gap is no longer a matter of risk appetite. Every public body was required to have a privacy management program in place by 11 June 2026, and the regulation made under the Protection of Privacy Act prescribes what that program must contain — including internal policies and procedures for how automated systems use personal information. If staff are putting personal information into an AI tool, the AI element is a prescribed part of a program that was already due. That date has passed.

Writing the policy is the easier half of closing it. A policy states rules; rules only govern behaviour once somebody has written down the steps for following them — who to ask, what gets logged, how long approval takes, what happens at 4pm on a Friday when something has already gone into the wrong tool. That second document is what turns an adopted policy into something staff can actually follow.

Document one

The AI Adoption Policy

The governing instrument. Written in plain language for the people who have to vote on it, and grounded in the legislation that actually applies to you.

01
Purpose and scope
Why the policy exists, who it binds — staff, contractors, and council or board — and which systems and information it governs.
02
Sanctioned and prohibited uses
Which AI tools may be used and for what, and the uses that are prohibited outright — above all, entering personal or confidential information into a public AI tool.
03
Data handling rules
How personal information may and may not be used with AI, written to your legislation and any data-residency obligation it carries.
04
Breach reporting obligations
What counts as a reportable breach involving AI, and where the obligation to report comes from.
05
Assigned accountability
The named role responsible for the policy, for approving tools, and for ongoing oversight — the leadership accountability a Privacy Management Program requires.
06
Mandatory training
Training as a condition of AI use, not an optional extra, so the rules reach the people expected to follow them.
Document two

The AI Adoption Procedures

The operating companion. This is the document a staff member opens on a Tuesday afternoon when they have a tool in front of them and a decision to make.

01
Requesting a tool
How a staff member asks for a new AI tool, what they have to supply with the request, and how long an answer takes. A request path with no stated timeline is how shadow AI starts.
02
The approval review
What the reviewer actually checks before saying yes — where the vendor stores data, what it does with inputs, whether it meets your residency obligations — and who signs off.
03
Logging and records
What gets written down, where it lives, and who can produce it if an oversight body or a client asks. Demonstrating compliance requires a record, not a recollection.
04
Breach response steps
The literal sequence for the moment someone realises personal information went into a public tool: who they tell, within what window, what gets contained, and who decides whether it is reportable. Written to be followed by someone who is alarmed and not thinking clearly.
05
Maintaining the sanctioned tool list
Who owns the approved list, how often it is reviewed, and how a tool is added or removed — so the list still means something a year after adoption.
06
Review and revision
When the procedures get revisited, and the point at which a change is significant enough that it belongs back in the policy for formal adoption.
Why two documents

They change at different speeds.

This is the practical argument, and it is the one that matters most to a CAO or an owner. A council adopts the policy. If the approved tool list lives inside that policy, then adding one tool means going back to council — agenda item, report, meeting, resolution — for a decision that should take an afternoon.

Separating them fixes that. The policy is stable and carries the authority. It says a sanctioned tool list exists, names who maintains it, and sets the standard a tool has to meet. The procedures hold the operational detail and can be revised by administration as the market moves, without diluting anything council approved.

The same logic applies in a business, with a board or an owner in place of a council. The document that gets formally signed off should be the one that does not need to change every quarter.

Two audiences

A municipality and a business need different documents.

The structure above holds in both cases. What changes is the law behind it, the approval chain, and the length.

For a municipality, the policy is written to your provincial privacy legislation — ATIA and POPA in Alberta, FOIPPA in British Columbia, MFIPPA in Ontario, FIPPA in Manitoba, LAFOIP in Saskatchewan — and framed for council adoption and a provincial oversight body. It forms part of the Privacy Management Program your legislation requires.

For a business, the driver is client information rather than citizen information: PIPEDA or your provincial private-sector privacy law, plus whatever confidentiality you have signed up to in your own contracts. The documents are shorter and there is no council to satisfy — but enterprise customers, insurers, and RFPs increasingly ask whether a written AI policy exists, and that is a poor question to be answering for the first time with a contract on the table.

Alberta municipalities

What POPA actually requires — and what it doesn’t.

Alberta’s Protection of Privacy Act and Access to Information Act replaced FOIP on 11 June 2025. Most of the attention went to the Act. The obligation that reaches AI sits in the regulation underneath it.

01
The privacy management program was due 11 June 2026
POPA s. 25(1) requires every public body — municipalities, municipal districts and counties included — to “establish and implement a privacy management program consisting of documented policies and procedures.” It must be proportional to the volume and sensitivity of the personal information held, and s. 25(2)(b) requires it to “comply with the prescribed requirements” — the clause that pulls in the regulation below. Public bodies were given one year from the Act coming into force; that year is up. And under s. 25(3), any person may request a copy of the program, which the public body must provide within 30 business days.
02
The regulation prescribes an automated-systems policy inside it
The Protection of Privacy (Ministerial) Regulation, Alta Reg 143/2025, s. 6(1)(b)(iii) requires that program to include policies and procedures for “how automated systems will use personal information, including any security or technical safeguards that will be implemented to protect personal information” — where the public body uses personal information in an automated system to generate content or make decisions, recommendations or predictions. A municipality whose staff have started using AI on resident information is inside that clause.
03
Highly sensitive information raises the bar again
Section 6(2)(a)(v) adds, for a public body holding a high volume of personal information or highly sensitive personal information, policies on “the use of personal information in artificial intelligence systems.” The regulation deems biometric information, financial information, and information about minors, seniors and vulnerable individuals to be highly sensitive — categories a municipality holds as a matter of routine.
04
And a privacy impact assessment may have to go to the Commissioner
Section 7(5)(e) requires a privacy impact assessment to be submitted to the Office of the Information and Privacy Commissioner where a practice, program, project or service “involves the development or use of innovative technology.” Standing up an AI tool will ordinarily engage that — which is a step better taken before deployment than after a complaint.

What the legislation does not do is name an AI policy. No Alberta statute requires a municipality to adopt one, and POPA contains no data-residency provision — the Commissioner has recommended that the province adopt an AI framework, which tells you there isn’t one yet. If a vendor tells you the law requires you to buy an AI policy, ask them for the section number.

The obligation is real; it is simply not the one it is usually described as. An AI adoption policy and its procedures are how a municipality satisfies a prescribed component of the privacy management program it is already required to hold. That is why we write them to sit inside that program — cross-referenced to your existing privacy policy, your privacy officer, and your PIA process — rather than as a standalone document parked beside it.

As of August 2026, the Got AI Policy registry listed 13 of 276 tracked Alberta municipalities as having publicly published an AI policy, with 28 more recorded as in progress. A municipality can have adopted a policy without posting it, so treat that as the published record rather than a census. On the published record, most of Alberta has not closed this.

Common questions

AI adoption policy questions

What is the difference between an AI policy and AI procedures?

A policy states the rules and who is accountable for them — it is the document council or leadership formally adopts. Procedures state the steps people take to follow those rules: how a tool gets requested and approved, what is logged, and what happens when something goes wrong. A policy without procedures is a statement of intent; procedures without a policy have no authority behind them.

Why should they be separate documents?

Because they change at different speeds and are approved by different people. A council adopts the policy and should not have to reconvene every time a tool is added to the approved list. Keeping the procedures separate lets administration maintain them as tools change, while the governing policy stays stable.

Does our existing IT acceptable use policy cover AI?

Generally no. An IT acceptable use policy that mentions AI does not document how personal information is handled across the organization, does not demonstrate leadership accountability, and does not specify AI-specific breach response. For a Canadian municipality it does not satisfy Privacy Management Program requirements under provincial legislation.

Do businesses need this too?

Businesses are not under municipal privacy legislation, but they hold client information subject to PIPEDA or provincial private-sector privacy law, and they carry confidentiality obligations in their own contracts. Enterprise customers and RFPs increasingly ask whether a written AI policy exists. The documents are shorter than a municipality’s, but the structure is the same.

Does Alberta law require a municipality to have an AI policy?

Not by that name — no Alberta statute requires a municipality to adopt an AI policy, and any vendor who tells you otherwise should be asked for the section number. What Alberta law does require is a privacy management program. POPA s. 25 obliged every public body to establish and implement one by 11 June 2026, and s. 6(1)(b)(iii) of the Protection of Privacy (Ministerial) Regulation, Alta Reg 143/2025, prescribes that the program include policies and procedures for how automated systems will use personal information. If your staff are using AI on personal information, that element is a prescribed part of a program that was already due.

What was the June 11, 2026 deadline?

ATIA and POPA replaced FOIP on 11 June 2025, and public bodies were given one year to establish and implement a privacy management program. The deadline was 11 June 2026 and it has passed. Municipalities, municipal districts and counties are public bodies for this purpose.

How does the AI policy fit into our privacy management program?

As a component of it, not a separate initiative. Alta Reg 143/2025 s. 6 prescribes what the program must contain, and automated systems and artificial intelligence appear in that list twice. Written properly, the AI adoption policy cross-references your existing privacy policy, your privacy officer designation, your security classification system and your PIA process — so the program reads as one instrument rather than two documents that quietly contradict each other.

How long does it take?

A focused engagement produces both documents in weeks rather than months. For a municipality the binding constraint is usually the council meeting calendar, not the drafting.

Can you work from a draft we have already started?

Yes. Many organizations have a draft or a downloaded template partway through. That is a reasonable starting point — the work is checking it against the legislation that actually applies, closing the gaps, and writing the procedures that were almost certainly never drafted alongside it.

Who owns the documents when you’re finished?

You do. They are delivered as your documents, under your name, for you to adopt, amend, and maintain. Spencer Morley Consulting is the contractor behind them, not a party to them.
